A geospatial look at how temperature, precipitation, and drought have shifted since 1940 — and what the next few decades may hold

Motivation

The Nebraska Sand Hills are the largest sand dune formation in the Western Hemisphere — roughly 20,000 square miles of grass-stabilized dunes in north-central Nebraska. They look like nothing else in the Great Plains: instead of flat cropland, the Sand Hills are rolling grassland held together almost entirely by the roots of native grasses. If that grass cover is ever lost — to drought, overgrazing, or fire — the sand underneath is free to blow, and the region can revert to the active dune field it was a few thousand years ago. That makes the Sand Hills one of the most climate-sensitive rangelands in North America: its stability depends on a fairly narrow balance between how much water the system receives and how much water it loses to heat and evaporation.

This matters far beyond scenery. The Sand Hills sit on top of the Ogallala Aquifer, support one of the country’s largest cow-calf cattle industries, and provide critical habitat for grassland birds and pollinators whose populations are declining across the Great Plains. Because the region’s vegetation, hydrology, and land use are all tightly coupled to climate, understanding how the climate here has already changed — and how it is projected to change further — is a first, necessary step toward answering harder ecological questions, such as how plant populations, forage production, and grassland resilience will respond in the coming decades.

This post builds that climate foundation. It combines an 86-year (1940–2025) observational climate record with a 30-year (2045–2074) future climate projection for the Sand Hills, and it uses both to characterize long-term trends and drought dynamics for the region. The workflow itself — extracting an ecoregion boundary, pulling gridded climate data, computing a drought index, testing for trends, and bias-correcting a downscaled climate model — is also a template that can be reused for any other landscape.

Research Questions

This analysis is organized around four questions:

  1. Has temperature in the Sand Hills changed detectably over the past ~85 years, and if so, in which seasons is the change strongest?
  2. Has precipitation changed over the same period, and has any change been enough to alter water availability, independent of temperature?
  3. Has the region’s drought status — a joint measure of water supply (precipitation) and water demand (heat-driven evaporative loss) — shifted across three roughly 30-year “generations” of climate (1941–1970, 1971–2000, 2001–2025)?
  4. Under a moderate-emissions (SSP2-4.5) and a high-emissions (SSP5-8.5) future climate scenario, how much additional warming is the Sand Hills projected to experience by the middle of this century (2045–2074), relative to its historical baseline?

Together, these four questions move from what has already happened (observed trends) to what is projected to happen next (future scenarios), which is the standard structure for a regional climate vulnerability assessment.

Data and Methods, in Plain Language

Before diving into code, here is a short glossary of the key ingredients used throughout this analysis. Skip ahead if you’re already familiar with these.

Note

  • EPA Level III Ecoregion: The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency divides the country into nested “ecoregions” based on shared geology, soils, vegetation, and climate. “Nebraska Sand Hills” is one such Level III ecoregion, and its official boundary (a shapefile) is what defines the study area used throughout this post.

  • PRISM climate data — A gridded (4-km resolution), monthly climate dataset produced by Oregon State University that reconstructs precipitation and temperature across the U.S. back to 1895 using thousands of weather stations plus elevation-aware interpolation. It is the closest thing available to a continuous, spatially complete climate record for the Sand Hills, and it is used here as the observational baseline.

  • SPEI (Standardized Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index): A drought index that compares how much water a region received (precipitation) to how much water it lost to the atmosphere through heat-driven evaporation and plant transpiration (potential evapotranspiration, or PET). A positive SPEI means wetter-than-typical conditions for that location and time window; a negative SPEI means drier-than-typical (drought) conditions. Unlike an index based on precipitation alone, SPEI accounts for the fact that a hotter atmosphere pulls more moisture out of the landscape even if rainfall stays the same — which is exactly the mechanism that makes future drought risk hard to predict from rainfall trends alone.

  • Sen’s slope and the Mann-Kendall test: Two classic, “robust” statistical tools for detecting a long-term trend in noisy, year-to-year data (like annual temperature or precipitation) without assuming the data follow a bell curve. Sen’s slope estimates the rate of change per year (e.g., °C per year); the Mann-Kendall test’s p-value tells us whether that trend is statistically distinguishable from random year-to-year noise. A p-value below 0.05 is the conventional cutoff for “the trend is unlikely to be a fluke.”

  • LOCA2 downscaled climate projections: Global climate models (GCMs) simulate the whole planet at a coarse resolution (~100 km grid cells), which is too coarse to say much about a specific county-sized region. LOCA2 is a statistical “downscaling” method that translates those coarse global simulations down to a fine 6-km grid over North America, preserving the model’s large-scale warming signal while adding realistic local detail. Here, one GCM (ACCESS-CM2, developed by Australia’s CSIRO and Bureau of Meteorology) is used as an illustrative single-model projection.

  • SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5: “Shared Socioeconomic Pathways” describe alternative futures for global greenhouse gas emissions. SSP2-4.5 is a moderate emissions pathway (emissions roughly plateau mid-century); SSP5-8.5 is a high emissions pathway (emissions keep rising). They are not predictions — they are “what if” scenarios used to bracket a plausible range of future warming.

  • Bias correction: Climate models don’t reproduce the real historical climate perfectly; a given model might run systematically a few degrees too warm or too cool in a specific location. Bias correction fixes this by comparing the model’s own historical simulation to the real observational record (PRISM, in this case) for the same historical period, computing the average offset (“bias”), and then applying that same offset to the model’s future projection. This puts the future projection on the same numerical scale as the historical observations, so the two can be compared directly.

The full pipeline below is built entirely in R, using sf and terra for spatial data, prism for climate data access, SPEI for drought index computation, trend for trend testing, and the tidyverse for data wrangling and visualization.

Part 3 — A Drought Index That Combines Rainfall and Heat (SPEI)

Rainfall totals alone don’t fully capture drought risk, because a hotter atmosphere evaporates more water from soil and plants even when rainfall is unchanged. SPEI addresses this by computing a monthly water balance (precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration, or PET) and then standardizing that water balance against its own long-term distribution, separately at several timescales (1, 3, 6, and 12 months). A 12-month SPEI, for example, reflects water balance conditions accumulated over the trailing year — useful for characterizing longer, slow-building droughts rather than a single dry month.

Part 4 — Has It Been Getting Warmer? Seasonal Temperature Trends

To ask “has it warmed” in a way that’s statistically defensible (rather than eyeballing a noisy year-to-year line), this section:

  1. Averages temperature spatially across the whole Sand Hills for every month, producing one regional monthly time series.
  2. Aggregates that monthly series into six seasonal/annual definitions (Winter, Spring, Summer, Fall, the May–September growing season, and the October–April non-growing season).
  3. Fits a Sen’s slope trend line to each season’s yearly values and tests significance with the Mann-Kendall test.
Show the R code 
library(dplyr)
library(tidyr)
library(ggplot2)
library(purrr)
library(trend)

# ---- 1. Collapse the pixel grid into one regional-average monthly series ----
regional_tmean <- climate_bind %>%
  group_by(Year, Month) %>%
  summarise(tmean = mean(tmean, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

# ---- 2. Define each season. `wrap = TRUE` marks seasons that cross the
#          Dec/Jan calendar-year boundary (e.g., Winter = Dec-Jan-Feb), so a
#          December value gets assigned to the FOLLOWING winter's year label
#          rather than splitting one winter across two calendar years. ----
season_defs <- list(
  Winter            = list(months = c(12, 1, 2),               wrap = TRUE),
  Spring            = list(months = c(3, 4, 5),                wrap = FALSE),
  Summer            = list(months = c(6, 7, 8),                wrap = FALSE),
  Fall              = list(months = c(9, 10, 11),               wrap = FALSE),
  Growing.season    = list(months = c(5, 6, 7, 8, 9),          wrap = FALSE),  # May-Sep
  NonGrowing.season = list(months = c(10, 11, 12, 1, 2, 3, 4), wrap = TRUE)    # Oct-Apr
)
season_levels <- names(season_defs)

# ---- 3. Helper: collapse monthly values into one value per season-year ----
compute_seasonal_annual <- function(data, value_col, months, wrap, agg = "mean") {
  d <- data %>%
    select(Year, Month, value = all_of(value_col)) %>%
    filter(Month %in% months)

  if (wrap) {
    late_months <- months[months > 6]
    d <- d %>% mutate(season_year = if_else(Month %in% late_months, Year + 1L, Year))
  } else {
    d <- d %>% mutate(season_year = Year)
  }

  d %>%
    group_by(season_year) %>%
    summarise(n_months = n(),
              value = if (agg == "sum") sum(value, na.rm = TRUE) else mean(value, na.rm = TRUE),
              .groups = "drop") %>%
    filter(n_months == length(months)) %>%   # drop partial seasons at either end of the record
    rename(Year = season_year)
}

# ---- 4. Build one long table with an annual value for every season ----
variables1 <- list(tmean = list(col = "tmean", label = "Temperature", units = "\u00b0C", agg = "mean"))

annual_tmean <- purrr::map_dfr(names(season_defs), function(season_name) {
  sd <- season_defs[[season_name]]
  purrr::map_dfr(names(variables1), function(var_name) {
    vd <- variables1[[var_name]]
    compute_seasonal_annual(regional_tmean, vd$col, sd$months, sd$wrap, agg = vd$agg) %>%
      mutate(Season = season_name, Variable = var_name)
  })
}) %>%
  mutate(Season = factor(Season, levels = season_levels))

# ---- 5. Fit Sen's slope + Mann-Kendall p-value, one trend per season ----
sens_results1 <- annual_tmean %>%
  group_by(Season) %>%
  group_modify(~{
    x <- .x %>% arrange(Year) %>% pull(value)
    ss <- trend::sens.slope(x)   # Sen's slope estimator (robust to outliers)
    data.frame(
      slope        = unname(ss$estimates),   # deg C per year
      p_value      = ss$p.value,              # Mann-Kendall significance test
      median_year  = median(.x$Year),
      median_value = median(x),
      min_year     = min(.x$Year),
      max_year     = max(.x$Year)
    )
  }) %>%
  ungroup()

# ---- 6. Build the fitted trend line (a straight line through the median
#          point, with slope = Sen's slope) for plotting ----
sens_line1 <- sens_results1 %>%
  rowwise() %>%
  mutate(data = list(tibble(
    Year  = c(min_year, max_year),
    value = median_value + slope * (c(min_year, max_year) - median_year)
  ))) %>%
  ungroup() %>%

This first attempt compares PRISM’s mean temperature (tmean) to the model’s maximum temperature (tasmax) — two genuinely different physical quantities, not two versions of the same one. The result is a monthly “bias correction” of roughly –6.6°C to –7.6°C, which is far too large to be a sensible bias correction; it is mostly just the average gap between a day’s high temperature and its daily mean. Comparing tasmax against tmean like this would silently bake a several-degree error into every future projection. The fix, carried out in the next section, is to build a proper tmean for the LOCA2 model too — by averaging its tasmax and tasmin — and compare that apples-to-apples against PRISM’s tmean.

Results Summary

Bringing the four research questions back together:

  1. Observed temperature trend (1940–2025): the Sen’s-slope/Mann-Kendall analysis in Part 4 shows season-by-season whether Sand Hills temperatures have shifted detectably over the past ~85 years; exact slopes and p-values are annotated directly on each panel of that figure.
  2. Observed precipitation trend (1940–2025): the parallel analysis in Part 5 shows the same for precipitation; as is typical for Great Plains rainfall, seasonal precipitation trends are generally noisier and less consistently significant than the temperature trends.
  3. Drought status across three climate generations (1941–2025): the SPEI12 period averages computed in Part 6 shift from a range of –0.46 to +0.19 (1941–1970), to –0.29 to +0.30 (1971–2000), to –0.16 to +0.59 (2001–2025) — a gradual, spatially explicit trend toward wetter conditions on balance across the record, with the accompanying difference maps showing exactly where that shift has been strongest.
  4. Projected mid-century warming (2045–2074): after correcting an initial bias-correction error (comparing daily maximum temperature to mean temperature, Part 7) and fixing it by deriving a proper model tmean (Part 8), both emissions scenarios project further warming above the historical baseline in every calendar month and across the full Sand Hills domain, with SSP5-8.5 (high emissions) consistently warmer than SSP2-4.5 (moderate emissions), as expected from the underlying physics of the two scenarios.

Conclusions

The Nebraska Sand Hills have already experienced measurable climate variation over the past 85 years, and the region’s own downscaled climate projections — built and bias-corrected from scratch in this post — point to continued warming through mid-century regardless of which emissions pathway plays out, with the magnitude of that warming depending substantially on which pathway occurs. On the water-availability side, the SPEI-based drought record shows no signal of a long-term drying trend at the ecoregion scale over 1941–2025; if anything, the multi-decadal average has trended slightly wetter, even as year-to-year variability and a warming atmosphere continue to raise the demand side of the water balance. For a grass-stabilized dune landscape whose long-term stability depends on maintaining continuous vegetation cover, this combination — rising temperatures (and therefore rising evaporative demand) alongside a precipitation record that has not clearly declined — is exactly the kind of climate trajectory that matters most for future work connecting this climate record to plant demography, forage production, and grassland resilience in the Sand Hills.

Limitations and Next Steps

  • Single global climate model. The future projections here rely on one GCM (ACCESS-CM2). Different GCMs disagree meaningfully on the magnitude (and sometimes even the sign) of regional precipitation change, even when they agree on the direction of warming. The process_one_model() function in Part 11 is written specifically so that additional GCMs can be added later, at which point the current single “line” for modeled historical and future temperature will become genuine multi-model uncertainty ribbons.
  • Temperature-only future projection. This post bias-corrects and projects future temperature; a full water-balance projection would also need bias-corrected future precipitation, so that a future SPEI (not just future temperature) could be computed and compared directly to the historical drought record in Part 6.
  • Spatial averaging in the trend tests. The Sen’s-slope/Mann-Kendall trend tests in Parts 4–5 are computed on a single, ecoregion-wide average time series. ## Motivation

The Nebraska Sand Hills are the largest sand dune formation in the Western Hemisphere — roughly 20,000 square miles of grass-stabilized dunes in north-central Nebraska. They look like nothing else in the Great Plains: instead of flat cropland, the Sand Hills are rolling grassland held together almost entirely by the roots of native grasses. If that grass cover is ever lost — to drought, overgrazing, or fire — the sand underneath is free to blow, and the region can revert to the active dune field it was a few thousand years ago. That makes the Sand Hills one of the most climate-sensitive rangelands in North America: its stability depends on a fairly narrow balance between how much water the system receives and how much water it loses to heat and evaporation.

This matters far beyond scenery. The Sand Hills sit on top of the Ogallala Aquifer, support one of the country's largest cow-calf cattle industries, and provide critical habitat for grassland birds and pollinators whose populations are declining across the Great Plains. Because the region's vegetation, hydrology, and land use are all tightly coupled to climate, understanding *how the climate here has already changed* — and *how it is projected to change further* — is a first, necessary step toward answering harder ecological questions, such as how plant populations, forage production, and grassland resilience will respond in the coming decades.

This post builds that climate foundation. It combines an 86-year (1940–2025) observational climate record with a 30-year (2045–2074) future climate projection for the Sand Hills, and it uses both to characterize long-term trends and drought dynamics for the region. The workflow itself — extracting an ecoregion boundary, pulling gridded climate data, computing a drought index, testing for trends, and bias-correcting a downscaled climate model — is also a template that can be reused for any other landscape.

## Research Questions

This analysis is organized around four questions:

1.  **Has temperature in the Sand Hills changed detectably over the past \~85 years, and if so, in which seasons is the change strongest?**
2.  **Has precipitation changed over the same period, and has any change been enough to alter water availability, independent of temperature?**
3.  **Has the region's *drought status* — a joint measure of water supply (precipitation) and water demand (heat-driven evaporative loss) — shifted across three roughly 30-year "generations" of climate (1941–1970, 1971–2000, 2001–2025)?**
4.  **Under a moderate-emissions (SSP2-4.5) and a high-emissions (SSP5-8.5) future climate scenario, how much additional warming is the Sand Hills projected to experience by the middle of this century (2045–2074), relative to its historical baseline?**

Together, these four questions move from *what has already happened* (observed trends) to *what is projected to happen next* (future scenarios), which is the standard structure for a regional climate vulnerability assessment.

## Data and Methods, in Plain Language

Before diving into code, here is a short glossary of the key ingredients used throughout this analysis. Skip ahead if you're already familiar with these.

## 

::: callout-note
- **EPA Level III Ecoregion**: The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency divides the country into nested "ecoregions" based on shared geology, soils, vegetation, and climate. "Nebraska Sand Hills" is one such Level III ecoregion, and its official boundary (a shapefile) is what defines the study area used throughout this post.

- **PRISM climate data** — A gridded (4-km resolution), monthly climate dataset produced by Oregon State University that reconstructs precipitation and temperature across the U.S. back to 1895 using thousands of weather stations plus elevation-aware interpolation. It is the closest thing available to a continuous, spatially complete climate record for the Sand Hills, and it is used here as the **observational baseline**.

- **SPEI (Standardized Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index)**: A drought index that compares how much water a region *received* (precipitation) to how much water it *lost* to the atmosphere through heat-driven evaporation and plant transpiration (potential evapotranspiration, or PET). A positive SPEI means wetter-than-typical conditions for that location and time window; a negative SPEI means drier-than-typical (drought) conditions. Unlike an index based on precipitation alone, SPEI accounts for the fact that a hotter atmosphere pulls more moisture out of the landscape even if rainfall stays the same — which is exactly the mechanism that makes future drought risk hard to predict from rainfall trends alone.

- **Sen's slope and the Mann-Kendall test**: Two classic, "robust" statistical tools for detecting a long-term trend in noisy, year-to-year data (like annual temperature or precipitation) without assuming the data follow a bell curve. Sen's slope estimates the *rate* of change per year (e.g., °C per year); the Mann-Kendall test's p-value tells us whether that trend is *statistically distinguishable from random year-to-year noise*. A p-value below 0.05 is the conventional cutoff for "the trend is unlikely to be a fluke."

- **LOCA2 downscaled climate projections**: Global climate models (GCMs) simulate the whole planet at a coarse resolution (\~100 km grid cells), which is too coarse to say much about a specific county-sized region. LOCA2 is a statistical "downscaling" method that translates those coarse global simulations down to a fine 6-km grid over North America, preserving the model's large-scale warming signal while adding realistic local detail. Here, one GCM (**ACCESS-CM2**, developed by Australia's CSIRO and Bureau of Meteorology) is used as an illustrative single-model projection.

- **SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5**: "Shared Socioeconomic Pathways" describe alternative futures for global greenhouse gas emissions. SSP2-4.5 is a **moderate** emissions pathway (emissions roughly plateau mid-century); SSP5-8.5 is a **high** emissions pathway (emissions keep rising). They are not predictions — they are "what if" scenarios used to bracket a plausible range of future warming.

- **Bias correction**: Climate models don't reproduce the real historical climate perfectly; a given model might run systematically a few degrees too warm or too cool in a specific location. Bias correction fixes this by comparing the model's *own* historical simulation to the real observational record (PRISM, in this case) for the same historical period, computing the average offset ("bias"), and then applying that same offset to the model's future projection. This puts the future projection on the same numerical scale as the historical observations, so the two can be compared directly.
:::

The full pipeline below is built entirely in **R**, using `sf` and `terra` for spatial data, `prism` for climate data access, `SPEI` for drought index computation, `trend` for trend testing, and the `tidyverse` for data wrangling and visualization.

## Part 4 — Has It Been Getting Warmer? Seasonal Temperature Trends

To ask "has it warmed" in a way that's statistically defensible (rather than eyeballing a noisy year-to-year line), this section:

1.  Averages temperature spatially across the whole Sand Hills for every month, producing one regional monthly time series.
2.  Aggregates that monthly series into six seasonal/annual definitions (Winter, Spring, Summer, Fall, the May–September growing season, and the October–April non-growing season).
3.  Fits a **Sen's slope** trend line to each season's yearly values and tests significance with the **Mann-Kendall test**.

```{r, warning=FALSE}
library(dplyr)
library(tidyr)
library(ggplot2)
library(purrr)
library(trend)

# ---- 1. Collapse the pixel grid into one regional-average monthly series ----
regional_tmean <- climate_bind %>%
  group_by(Year, Month) %>%
  summarise(tmean = mean(tmean, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

# ---- 2. Define each season. `wrap = TRUE` marks seasons that cross the
#          Dec/Jan calendar-year boundary (e.g., Winter = Dec-Jan-Feb), so a
#          December value gets assigned to the FOLLOWING winter's year label
#          rather than splitting one winter across two calendar years. ----
season_defs <- list(
  Winter            = list(months = c(12, 1, 2),               wrap = TRUE),
  Spring            = list(months = c(3, 4, 5),                wrap = FALSE),
  Summer            = list(months = c(6, 7, 8),                wrap = FALSE),
  Fall              = list(months = c(9, 10, 11),               wrap = FALSE),
  Growing.season    = list(months = c(5, 6, 7, 8, 9),          wrap = FALSE),  # May-Sep
  NonGrowing.season = list(months = c(10, 11, 12, 1, 2, 3, 4), wrap = TRUE)    # Oct-Apr
)
season_levels <- names(season_defs)

# ---- 3. Helper: collapse monthly values into one value per season-year ----
compute_seasonal_annual <- function(data, value_col, months, wrap, agg = "mean") {
  d <- data %>%
    select(Year, Month, value = all_of(value_col)) %>%
    filter(Month %in% months)

  if (wrap) {
    late_months <- months[months > 6]
    d <- d %>% mutate(season_year = if_else(Month %in% late_months, Year + 1L, Year))
  } else {
    d <- d %>% mutate(season_year = Year)
  }

  d %>%
    group_by(season_year) %>%
    summarise(n_months = n(),
              value = if (agg == "sum") sum(value, na.rm = TRUE) else mean(value, na.rm = TRUE),
              .groups = "drop") %>%
    filter(n_months == length(months)) %>%   # drop partial seasons at either end of the record
    rename(Year = season_year)
}

# ---- 4. Build one long table with an annual value for every season ----

    compute_seasonal_annual(regional_tmean, vd$col, sd$months, sd$wrap, agg = vd$agg) %>%
      mutate(Season = season_name, Variable = var_name)
  })
}) %>%

      p_value      = ss$p.value,              # Mann-Kendall significance test

      median_value = median(x),

## Part 5 — Has It Been Getting Wetter or Drier? Seasonal Precipitation Trends

The same Sen's-slope / Mann-Kendall approach is repeated here for precipitation, using seasonal *totals* (sum of monthly rainfall) rather than seasonal *averages*, since it makes more physical sense to talk about "how much rain fell this season" than "the average monthly rainfall rate."

```{r, warning=FALSE}
library(dplyr)
library(purrr)
library(ggplot2)
library(trend)

# ---- 1. Regional-average monthly precipitation series ----
regional_ppt <- climate_bind %>%
  group_by(Year, Month) %>%
  summarise(ppt_mm = mean(ppt_mm, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

# ---- 2. Same season definitions as Part 4 ----
season_defs <- list(
  Winter     = list(months = c(12, 1, 2),               wrap = TRUE),
  Spring     = list(months = c(3, 4, 5),                 wrap = FALSE),
  Summer     = list(months = c(6, 7, 8),                 wrap = FALSE),
  Fall       = list(months = c(9, 10, 11),                wrap = FALSE),
  Growing    = list(months = c(5, 6, 7, 8, 9),           wrap = FALSE),  # May-Sep
  NonGrowing = list(months = c(10, 11, 12, 1, 2, 3, 4),  wrap = TRUE)    # Oct-Apr
)
season_levels <- names(season_defs)

# ---- 3. Same seasonal-aggregation helper as Part 4 ----
compute_seasonal_annual <- function(data, value_col, months, wrap, agg = "mean") {
  d <- data %>%
    select(Year, Month, value = all_of(value_col)) %>%
    filter(Month %in% months)

  if (wrap) {
    late_months <- months[months > 6]
    d <- d %>% mutate(season_year = if_else(Month %in% late_months, Year + 1L, Year))
  } else {
    d <- d %>% mutate(season_year = Year)
  }

  d %>%
    group_by(season_year) %>%
    summarise(n_months = n(),
              value = if (agg == "sum") sum(value, na.rm = TRUE) else mean(value, na.rm = TRUE),
              .groups = "drop") %>%
    filter(n_months == length(months)) %>%
    rename(Year = season_year)
}

# ---- 4. Build the long annual precipitation table (using SUM, not mean) ----
variables <- list(ppt_mm = list(col = "ppt_mm", label = "Precipitation", units = "mm", agg = "sum"))

  sd <- season_defs[[season_name]]
  purrr::map_dfr(names(variables), function(var_name) {
    vd <- variables[[var_name]]
    compute_seasonal_annual(regional_ppt, vd$col, sd$months, sd$wrap, agg = vd$agg) %>%
      mutate(Season = season_name, Variable = var_name)
  })
}) %>%
  mutate(Season = factor(Season, levels = season_levels))

  group_by(Season) %>%

      slope        = unname(ss$estimates),   # mm per year
      p_value      = ss$p.value,
      median_year  = median(.x$Year),
      median_value = median(x),
      min_year     = min(.x$Year),
      max_year     = max(.x$Year)
    )
  }) %>%
  ungroup()

# ---- 6. Trend line through the medians ----
sens_line <- sens_results %>%
  rowwise() %>%
  mutate(data = list(tibble(
    Year  = c(min_year, max_year),
    value = median_value + slope * (c(min_year, max_year) - median_year)
  ))) %>%
  ungroup() %>%
  select(Season, data) %>%
  tidyr::unnest(data)

## Part 6 — Mapping Drought Across Three Climate "Generations" (1941–2025)

Rather than looking at one regional-average number, this section maps the **spatial pattern** of long-term drought conditions (12-month SPEI) across the Sand Hills, and asks whether that pattern has shifted across three successive \~30-year climate periods: 1941–1970, 1971–2000, and 2001–2025. Thirty years is the conventional length of a "climate normal" period, so comparing three of them is a standard way to describe a long-term climatic shift.

```{r, warning=FALSE}
library(dplyr)
library(tidyr)
library(terra)

crs_string <- "EPSG:9822"   # the coordinate reference system of the PRISM/SPEI grid

# 1) Collapse monthly SPEI12 down to one annual value per pixel
df_annual <- df %>%
  group_by(x, y, Year) %>%
  summarise(spei12_annual = mean(spei12, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

# 2) Assign each year to one of three 30-year "climate generations"
df_annual <- df_annual %>%
  mutate(period = case_when(
    Year >= 1941 & Year <= 1970 ~ "1941_1970",
    Year >= 1971 & Year <= 2000 ~ "1971_2000",
    Year >= 2001 & Year <= 2025 ~ "2001_2025",
    TRUE ~ NA_character_
  )) %>%
  filter(!is.na(period))

# 3) Average SPEI12 within each period, per pixel — this collapses ~30 years
#    of monthly detail into a single "typical drought status" value per pixel
#    per period

  group_by(x, y, period) %>%
  summarise(spei12_mean = mean(spei12_annual, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

crs(r) <- crs_string

**Interpreting the actual numbers:** the printed raster summary above shows the SPEI12 period-average range shifting from **–0.46 to +0.19** in 1941–1970, to **–0.29 to +0.30** in 1971–2000, to **–0.16 to +0.59** in 2001–2025. In other words, across all three periods the *lowest* period-average SPEI value has been getting less negative (drought conditions less extreme in their worst pixels) and the *highest* period-average value has been getting more positive (wet conditions more pronounced in their best pixels). Because SPEI is standardized to have a long-run mean of zero, a 30-year period whose average sits above zero is, on balance, wetter than the multi-decade norm; a period below zero is, on balance, drier. Read together, this progression is consistent with a gradual **wetting trend** across the Sand Hills SPEI12 record over the past 85 years — though it says nothing on its own about whether any individual pixel's shift is statistically significant. The difference maps make this spatially explicit: they show precisely which parts of the Sand Hills became wetter or drier between each pair of periods, rather than relying on one basin-wide number.

## Part 7 — Looking Ahead: Building a Future Climate Projection

The next several sections shift from "what has already happened" to "what a climate model projects for the future." We already have an 86-year historical PRISM record for the Sand Hills. To compare that against a plausible future, three things are needed, in order:

::: callout-warning
## Catching a real methodological mistake

This first attempt compares PRISM's **mean** temperature (`tmean`) to the model's **maximum** temperature (`tasmax`) — two genuinely different physical quantities, not two versions of the same one. The result is a monthly "bias correction" of roughly **–6.6°C to –7.6°C**, which is far too large to be a sensible bias correction; it is mostly just the average gap between a day's high temperature and its daily mean. Comparing `tasmax` against `tmean` like this would silently bake a several-degree error into every future projection. The fix, carried out in the next section, is to build a proper `tmean` for the LOCA2 model too — by averaging its `tasmax` and `tasmin` — and compare that apples-to-apples against PRISM's `tmean`.
:::

## Results Summary

Bringing the four research questions back together:

1.  **Observed temperature trend (1940–2025):** the Sen's-slope/Mann-Kendall analysis in Part 4 shows season-by-season whether Sand Hills temperatures have shifted detectably over the past \~85 years; exact slopes and p-values are annotated directly on each panel of that figure.
2.  **Observed precipitation trend (1940–2025):** the parallel analysis in Part 5 shows the same for precipitation; as is typical for Great Plains rainfall, seasonal precipitation trends are generally noisier and less consistently significant than the temperature trends.
3.  **Drought status across three climate generations (1941–2025):** the SPEI12 period averages computed in Part 6 shift from a range of –0.46 to +0.19 (1941–1970), to –0.29 to +0.30 (1971–2000), to –0.16 to +0.59 (2001–2025) — a gradual, spatially explicit trend toward wetter conditions on balance across the record, with the accompanying difference maps showing exactly where that shift has been strongest.
4.  **Projected mid-century warming (2045–2074):** after correcting an initial bias-correction error (comparing daily maximum temperature to mean temperature, Part 7) and fixing it by deriving a proper model `tmean` (Part 8), both emissions scenarios project further warming above the historical baseline in every calendar month and across the full Sand Hills domain, with SSP5-8.5 (high emissions) consistently warmer than SSP2-4.5 (moderate emissions), as expected from the underlying physics of the two scenarios.

## Conclusions

The Nebraska Sand Hills have already experienced measurable climate variation over the past 85 years, and the region's own downscaled climate projections — built and bias-corrected from scratch in this post — point to continued warming through mid-century regardless of which emissions pathway plays out, with the magnitude of that warming depending substantially on which pathway occurs. On the water-availability side, the SPEI-based drought record shows no signal of a long-term drying trend at the ecoregion scale over 1941–2025; if anything, the multi-decadal average has trended slightly wetter, even as year-to-year variability and a warming atmosphere continue to raise the *demand* side of the water balance. For a grass-stabilized dune landscape whose long-term stability depends on maintaining continuous vegetation cover, this combination — rising temperatures (and therefore rising evaporative demand) alongside a precipitation record that has not clearly declined — is exactly the kind of climate trajectory that matters most for future work connecting this climate record to plant demography, forage production, and grassland resilience in the Sand Hills.

## Limitations and Next Steps

- **Single global climate model.** The future projections here rely on one GCM (ACCESS-CM2). Different GCMs disagree meaningfully on the magnitude (and sometimes even the sign) of regional precipitation change, even when they agree on the direction of warming. The `process_one_model()` function in Part 11 is written specifically so that additional GCMs can be added later, at which point the current single "line" for modeled historical and future temperature will become genuine multi-model uncertainty ribbons.
- **Temperature-only future projection.** This post bias-corrects and projects future temperature; a full water-balance projection would also need bias-corrected future precipitation, so that a future SPEI (not just future temperature) could be computed and compared directly to the historical drought record in Part 6.
- **Spatial averaging in the trend tests.** The Sen's-slope/Mann-Kendall trend tests in Parts 4–5 are computed on a single, ecoregion-wide average time series. The drought mapping in Part 6 shows that conditions are not perfectly spatially uniform, so a natural next step is repeating the trend tests pixel-by-pixel to map *where* the significant temperature and precipitation trends are strongest, rather than relying on one regional summary.
